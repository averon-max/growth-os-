## What is credit monitoring?

Credit monitoring is a service that watches for certain changes in your credit reports and notifies you when it detects them. Depending on the provider, it may track one, two, or all three nationwide credit bureaus—Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. It can help you notice an unfamiliar account, a new credit inquiry, or a change to information reported by a lender. **Monitoring helps you spot possible problems; it does not stop identity theft before it happens.** The [Federal Trade Commission (FTC) explains what credit monitoring services can and cannot detect](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-identity-theft).

### Credit monitoring, reports, and scores

A credit report is a record of credit accounts and payment-related information. A credit score is a number calculated using information from a credit report. **Credit monitoring, credit reports, and credit scores are related, but they are not the same thing.** A service that shows your score is not necessarily watching all three credit reports for changes. Likewise, receiving an alert does not automatically tell you why something changed.

Providers choose which credit bureaus and events their services cover. An alert may concern a new account, a credit inquiry, a reported late payment, a change to your credit limit, or updated personal information. Not every provider offers every type of alert. Before signing up, check the reports included, what triggers notifications, and whether the provider gives you access to the underlying report entry.

Monitoring can be useful if you want to know about certain changes without manually checking your reports each time. Some companies offer free credit monitoring alerts, while others charge a subscription. Neither option replaces the ability to review your own credit reports.  In the United States, you can also review your credit reports from each of the three nationwide bureaus **for free once a week** at [AnnualCreditReport.com](https://www.annualcreditreport.com/). The [CFPB explains this free alternative to a paid monitoring service](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-credit-monitoring-service-en-1365/).

## How does credit monitoring work?

Credit monitoring follows a simple sequence: you enroll, the provider watches the credit reports covered by your plan, and it sends an alert when it identifies a monitored change. Alerts may arrive by email, text, phone, or an in-app message, depending on the provider. **Notification speed varies by service and event**, so do not assume every change will be reported within a fixed number of hours.

### Enrollment, monitoring, and notifications

The process has three basic parts:

1. **Enrollment.** You choose a free or paid service, review its terms, and authorize any access required for monitoring. Check whether your plan covers Equifax, Experian, TransUnion, or a smaller selection.
2. **Monitoring.** The provider checks the reports or other credit information included in your plan. The events it looks for depend on the service; simply offering a credit score does not mean it detects every new account or inquiry.
3. **Notification and review.** If a covered change is identified, the provider sends an alert. You then open the report details and determine whether the activity is familiar, inaccurate, or potentially fraudulent.

For example, suppose an alert says that a new credit card account appeared on a monitored report. The alert identifies something to review, not proof that an identity thief opened the account. Compare the account name, opening date, and related details with your own records. If the information is unfamiliar, follow the investigation steps in the final section of this guide.

### Verify what is actually included in your service

**How do you know if you already have credit monitoring?** First, check for an enrollment confirmation, a service account, or a credit-monitoring benefit listed by your bank, credit card issuer, employer, insurer, or a company that notified you about a data breach. Then verify which reports and alerts are actually included. The FTC notes that monitoring can be provided through banks, credit unions, employers, insurers, and other benefits; its [identity-theft guide describes these arrangements](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-identity-theft). If you cannot confirm your enrollment, use the provider's official website or account support rather than trusting an unexpected link in an email.

**Is viewing your credit score the same as monitoring your credit?** No. Your bank or an app might show a periodically updated score without providing alerts about all three reports. Check the specific reporting and notification features instead of relying on the label “credit monitoring.”

## What are the limitations of credit monitoring?

Credit monitoring is a detection tool, not an all-purpose fraud-prevention system. It can bring covered changes to your attention, but it cannot guarantee that an identity thief will be stopped or that every misuse of personal information will appear on a credit report. The [FTC lists both the events these services may flag and activities they generally do not cover](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-identity-theft).

### What monitoring may miss or explain incompletely

A monitoring service might alert you to a new inquiry or account while offering little information about whether it is legitimate. Some plans cover only one bureau, and lenders do not necessarily report to every bureau at the same time. A service also may not monitor particular event categories. **A missing alert is not proof that no fraud occurred.**

Credit-report monitoring generally will not warn you when someone withdraws money from your bank account or uses your Social Security number to file a fraudulent tax return. Those problems involve different records and may require direct account review or other identity-protection steps. Broader “identity monitoring” features are separate from credit-report monitoring and should be evaluated on their own terms.

Free plans should not automatically be described as slower or less accurate than paid plans. Coverage, alert frequency, report access, and extra support differ by provider; read the actual plan details rather than assuming a paid subscription always catches more.

### Credit monitoring vs. a credit freeze

**Do you need credit monitoring if your credit is frozen?** Possibly, but the two tools have different jobs. A *security freeze* restricts access to your credit file by prospective creditors and generally makes it harder for someone else to open new credit in your name. *Monitoring* watches for covered changes and notifies you afterward. A freeze does not prevent every form of identity theft, and monitoring is not a substitute for a freeze.

You can **freeze and unfreeze your credit file for free** at each of the three nationwide bureaus. You need to request a freeze separately from Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. You can still review your reports while they are frozen, and companies you hire to monitor your credit are among the parties that may still have permitted access. See the [CFPB's explanation of credit freezes, permitted access, and free removal](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-credit-freeze-or-security-freeze-on-my-credit-report-en-1341/).

If you have a freeze, you can continue checking existing accounts and reports yourself. Whether additional paid monitoring is worthwhile depends on how much you value its alerts, which bureaus it covers, and what other protection you already have. When any alert arrives, inspect the underlying information rather than relying only on the notification.

## Does credit monitoring affect your credit score?

**No. Using a credit monitoring service to review your credit information does not, by itself, hurt your credit score.** Checking your own credit report is not a new-credit application and does not create a hard inquiry. The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau confirms that requesting your own report has no effect on your score](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/does-requesting-my-credit-report-hurt-my-credit-score-en-1229/).

### Soft inquiries versus hard inquiries

A *soft inquiry* may occur when you check your own report or when an existing lender reviews your account. Soft inquiries do not affect credit scores. A *hard inquiry* usually occurs when a lender checks your report after you apply for credit; it can affect your score. These are different events. Opening a monitoring dashboard or reading an alert should not be confused with applying for a loan. For details, see the [CFPB guide to hard and soft credit inquiries](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-credit-inquiry-en-1317/).

What if your monitoring app shows that your score fell? The service may have detected a change that was already reflected in your credit file, such as a new inquiry, a reported late payment, or updated account information. That does not mean the monitoring caused the change. Inspect the associated report entry and compare it with your records; if you do not recognize the activity, investigate it.

### Why the displayed score can differ

Also, **you have more than one credit score**. A service may display a different scoring model or use different bureau data than the lender reviewing your loan application. Two scores can differ even when neither contains an error. The [CFPB explains why different lenders and products use different credit scores](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/credit-reports-and-scores/understand-your-credit-score/).

In short, monitoring provides information about credit activity; it does not generate the underlying account activity. Treat the displayed score as a useful signal, and use your credit report to investigate a change that looks unfamiliar.

## What should you look for in a credit monitoring service?

Choose a service by its **coverage, alerts, report access, total cost, and limits**, not by a promise to “protect your identity” without explaining how. There is no universal package that works equally well for everyone.

### Compare bureau coverage and alert types

Start with bureau coverage. A plan might monitor one report, two reports, or all three nationwide reports. If a provider advertises a “3 bureau credit monitoring” plan, verify that each bureau is actually monitored and learn whether alerts and report access differ by bureau. Seeing a credit score from one bureau is not evidence that every bureau is covered.

Next, check what triggers an alert. Does the provider notify you about new accounts, hard inquiries, changes in account status, reported late payments, or changes to personal information? How frequently are the reports checked, and where can you inspect the entry behind a notification? The [FTC provides a practical list of questions to ask before buying monitoring](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-identity-theft).

### Free versus paid credit monitoring

**How much does credit monitoring cost?** Some services are free, while paid plans charge monthly or annual fees. There is no single standard price, and features vary substantially. The [CFPB reports that some services cost more than $15 per month](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-credit-monitoring-service-en-1365/); this is an example, not a price ceiling or a current quote for any specific provider. Check promotional periods, automatic renewals, cancellation rules, and whether credit-score access or identity-recovery assistance costs extra.

**Can you get credit monitoring for free?** Yes, some providers offer free monitoring and alerts, including offers made after certain data breaches. But getting a **free credit report** is not the same as receiving **automatic monitoring alerts**. US consumers can review the three nationwide credit reports weekly for free through [AnnualCreditReport.com](https://www.annualcreditreport.com/), even without subscribing to a monitoring product. The [FTC also explains free credit reports and monitoring offers](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/understanding-your-credit).

**Do you need credit monitoring, and is it worth paying for?** It depends on what you want it to do. If you already check your reports regularly and have frozen your credit, a paid alert subscription may offer limited additional value. If you prefer notifications between manual checks, have received a data-breach notice, or want help reviewing covered changes, a service may be useful. Before paying, compare the bureaus monitored, the types of alerts, the price after any promotion, and the assistance offered if suspicious activity appears. Monitoring cannot guarantee prevention, so it should complement—not replace—free reports, account security, and a security freeze where appropriate.

Finally, confirm the service's boundaries. Does the provider disclose the events it does not monitor? Can you see the report entry behind an alert? What happens if you cancel? A useful plan should make these answers easy to find before you enroll.

## What should you do after a credit monitoring alert?

An unexpected alert is a reason to check the underlying credit information, not automatic proof of fraud. **Start by identifying exactly what changed:** the account, lender, inquiry, balance, status, or personal-information entry. Compare it with applications you made and activity you recognize. Keep the alert and note the date, account name, and other relevant details.

### What to do when the activity is unfamiliar

Next, decide which of these situations applies:

1. **You recognize the activity.** The alert may simply reflect a legitimate account or an update. No dispute is necessary solely because the service sent a notification.
2. **The information looks wrong, but not necessarily fraudulent.** Review the report and gather records that show the correct information. The CFPB recommends disputing inaccuracies with both the credit reporting company and the business that provided the information. Follow its [credit-report dispute instructions](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-do-i-dispute-an-error-on-my-credit-report-en-314/).
3. **You suspect identity theft.** Contact the affected business's fraud department through its official contact information, review your other reports, and consider a fraud alert or security freeze. Use [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/Steps) to report identity theft and get a personalized recovery plan.

When you dispute inaccurate information, explain which item is wrong and why, and keep copies of relevant documents. The credit reporting company and the information furnisher have investigation duties; the outcome depends on what the investigation establishes. If the information is wrong or cannot be verified, it generally must be corrected or removed under the applicable rules. An unfamiliar creditor name is not always fraud, since a lender or collection company may appear under a name you do not immediately recognize. The [CFPB explains how to handle unfamiliar creditor names](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/should-i-dispute-a-creditor-on-my-credit-report-if-i-dont-recognize-the-company-en-1331/).

### Follow up after a dispute or identity-theft report

Continue monitoring after you act. A single alert may reveal only part of the problem, and a credit-monitoring service may not see misuse outside credit reports. The practical sequence is: **verify the report entry, document the issue, contact the right organization, and follow up on the resolution.**
